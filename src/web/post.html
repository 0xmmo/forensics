Small Model Forensics
viewerpost
2026-05-13 · @0xmmo

Claude and I made 2,000 API calls to nine small closed-weight models across three providers in a range of prompt sizes between 100 and 1M tokens. I wanted to determine the models with the best speed and latency for my application. But ended up discovering some interesting things about how providers scale inference, or fail to, in 2026.

You can view all the measurements in the interactive viewer. Code and raw dataset on GitHub.

01Every model's prefill is nowhere near O(n²)

Plot the minimum first-token latency (excluding network) against input size, log-log. Textbook dense attention models with O(n²) prefill would give you a line with slope near 1 at large n; every doubling of context should cost roughly double the prefill wall time. What we actually see is a much flatter curve. Even with >100K contexts where opaque provider overheads become negligible, prefill still scales sub-linearly.

Fit a power law C · n^α to the floor of each curve and read off the exponent.

Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite walks from 204 input tokens to 866 k input tokens — a factor of 4,200 in context — for only 0.7s → 5s in wall time. Seven times more latency for four thousand times more context. GPT-4.1 Nano exceeds 23s at half that context.

02Tokens are not created equal

Each provider uses the same tokenizer across all model versions, but across providers tokens are not apples to apples. Going from OpenAI to Anthropic will cost you 14% more input tokens for the same content.

4 chars per token is a good estimate for English text, but with our random prompt (hex-encoded random bytes), the ratio was closer to 1. Keep that in mind when you're sending high-entropy content.

04Gemini Lite goes the wrong way

Between 401 KB and 601 KB, the longer prompt is faster. End to end. Not noise.

The Lite curve doesn’t just bend — it dips. The TTFT floor at 256 KB is 1,181 ms. At 601 KB, with 2.3× more tokens, the floor is 912 ms. The longer prompt gets first token sooner.

You can argue with one data point. You can’t really argue with the scatter. Every individual call at 601 KB sits below the median of the 256 KB distribution. The lone 6.8 s spike at 400 KB is the only call that looks like it missed the routing change.

The cleanest way to look at the same story is to plot TTFT and total request latency on linear axes side by side. Both dip in the same band.

The simplest story is a routing transition somewhere near the 100 k-token band — the request gets handed to a different serving tier. By 601 KB it has cleanly landed on the new tier. By 1 MB it is still cheaper than at 256 KB.

A request at 601 KB completes its entire round trip — prefill plus decode of 96 tokens — in 1,148 ms at the floor. A request at 256 KB, with 2.3× fewer tokens, takes 1,704 ms. The longer request finishes first.

Flip the y-axis to throughput — tokens processed per second at the latency floor — and Lite’s curve does something genuinely strange. Throughput keeps climbing.

Throughput that rises with prompt size is the signature of a system amortising fixed costs over more work. It is not the signature of per-token compute that scales with context. Whatever Lite does past ~100 k tokens, the cost per token of doing it goes down.

05Decode is the new bottleneck

Everyone fixates on prefill. At a million bytes of context, decode is the part that ruins your day.

The textbook says decode-per-token should be near flat. Output is small, KV cache grows only on the output side, and per-step cost is dominated by the cache read plus a fixed-size compute. Maybe it rises a touch as the prefix grows. It shouldn’t fall, and it shouldn’t explode.

It does both. Different models, different directions.

The way to see how dangerous this is — for anyone shipping a product on top of these APIs — is to look at prefill as a fraction of total request time. On Lite at 1 MB, prefill is 88% of the call. Decode for 96 tokens costs only 240 ms. On gpt-5.4-mini at 1 MB, prefill is 24%. Decode for 100 tokens costs 10.4 s.

A practical version of the same fact: at 1 MB of context, bumping max_tokens from 100 to 1000 adds ~97 seconds on gpt-5.4-mini and ~3 seconds on Gemini Lite. If you’re building anything that needs long answers after long prompts, that one chart matters more than the entire TTFT story.

06The 50/50 split on random hex

Feed random hex with “write a story” appended. At temperature = 0, models split exactly half-and-half between “ok” and a near-capped 1,000-token story. No middle.

This one was an accident. We added random hex prompts as a tokenizer-stress test, then noticed the output-length distribution had a hole in the middle. Then we noticed that hole was a perfectly empty notch, and that several models sat at exactly 50/50 between very short and very long. At temperature = 0. Across n ≥ 60 repeats.

At temperature = 0, an exactly half-and-half split is the fingerprint of a model sitting at a near-perfect tie in the first-token logit. Two candidate continuations — something like "ok" and "Once upon a time" — with logits separated by less than the per-call nonce can perturb them. There is no third stable answer, so the distribution is bimodal with zero observations in the middle, no matter how many repeats we run.

The two exceptions are interesting in the opposite direction. gpt-4.1-mini and gpt-3.5-turbo always produce long output. Whatever training pushed the smaller and newer models toward a bail-out branch on random input has not been applied to those two. The larger model in this slice is more willing to engage with garbage, not less.

07What we think is going on

Six things you can take away from the dataset without overcommitting.

None of what follows is testable from the data we already have. Each becomes testable with a small, directed follow-up sweep — which is roughly the work plan that falls out of this post.

1. Nothing we measured looks like dense O(n²) attention. Every long-context route in the lineup is doing something else — block-sparse, retrieval-augmented, paged, hierarchical, something. The closest thing we have to a candidate for honest dense attention is gpt-4.1-nano at the top of its range (α ≈ 1.02), and even that is more likely a routing change than a kernel.

2. The “fastest model” question depends entirely on which slice of the curve you’re on. GPT-4.1-nano is the fastest sub-1KB model and the fifth-fastest at 1 MB. There is no model that is best everywhere. Any latency benchmark that only samples one slice is misleading at the others.

3. OpenAI hasn’t changed its tokenizer in years. Five model versions across at least three generations produce identical bytes-per-token numbers. That’s a deliberate choice: it lets prefix caches, embedding tables, and serving infrastructure ride across model versions without translation. Gemini and Anthropic each ship their own. Gemini’s in particular has aggressive byte-fallback that inflates random/binary content by ~3.6× relative to its English ratio.

4. Decode is the bigger story at 1 MB than prefill. A 33× gap on ms/tok turns into a 30-second difference on a 1,000-token response at the same context size. For products that need long answers after long prompts, the prefill curves are basically a distraction.

5. Gemini Lite past ~100 k tokens is a different machine. Lower TTFT, lower decode, higher throughput — all on the same model name, the same API, the same call shape. We have four candidate mechanisms (speculative decoding kicking in past a threshold; long-context requests landing on different hardware; a KV-cache layout switch; streaming-chunk coalescing distorting the denominator) and none of them are falsifiable from the data we have. Each becomes falsifiable with a small directed sweep.

6. Models facing random hex sit at a near-tie in the first-token logit. The 50/50 bimodal output is what you get when the per-call nonce is just enough to push the decision between “refuse” and “comply”. It’s a clean window into the temperature = 0 logit landscape under adversarial input, and a sign that frontier models have surprisingly flat decision surfaces when the prompt is meaningless.

The honest summary of this whole exercise: the providers are not running anything close to the same architecture under the same product names. You can’t reason about them as if they are. The right unit of analysis is the curve, not the number.
