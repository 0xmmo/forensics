Notes from the prefill floor

We measured time-to-first-token across seven frontier-tier models from one second to one million characters of context. Nothing scales like dense attention. Several curves carry reproducible humps at characteristic context sizes. A few models accelerate where they should slow down. Here is what the curves look like, where they bend, and the most honest set of guesses we have for why.

§1Method, briefly

The conceit of this project is small: if a closed model serves a million-token request in roughly the time it serves a thousand-token one, the people running it are doing something other than dense quadratic attention. We cannot peer into their serving stack, but we can curl their endpoint, ten thousand times, and let the wall clock tell us about the parts they were too modest to advertise.

We sweep input length on a roughly geometric grid from ~250 chars to ~1 M chars across three prompt shapes — song-toads (filler text), random (high-entropy tokens), and needle-p50 (a discriminative fact at the prompt midpoint). Trial order is randomised across (model, size, repeat); requests are issued strictly sequentially, one in flight at a time. Output is capped at max_tokens = 100, sampling at temperature = 0, all caching opted out, all reasoning budgets explicitly zeroed.

We report TTFT as the per-cell minimum, not the mean. A serving stack's 10th-percentile latency at a fixed input length is the closest thing we can observe to its compute floor. Means and tails are dominated by queueing, batching pile-ups, and provider-side noise that have nothing to do with the question we came here to ask.

One important caveat carries through everything below: token counts are not comparable across providers. Each provider tokenises its own way, so when we say "~100 k input tokens" on Gemini and the same on OpenAI we mean two different prompts. Within a single (provider, model), the comparison is honest. Across them, prefer the byte-count axis.

§2The shape of the curves

Sub-linear is the rule, not the exception. Nothing fits dense.

Before looking at any model in particular, the first thing to do is to fit a power law to the latency floor and read off the exponent. If TTFT scales as C · n^α with input size n, then the exponent α is the rough character of the prefill. Dense O(n²) attention asks for α ≈ 1.0 at large n. What we see is uniformly smaller than that — in some cases dramatically so.

The headline is the spread: at the high-context end, the fastest scaler is roughly 3.5× better than the slowest. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite walks ~204 tokens to ~866 k tokens — a factor of 4,200 in context — for only 726 ms → 5,027 ms on the floor. That is a factor of seven in wall time across four orders of magnitude in context. It is hard to call that attention.

Lite walks four thousand times more context for seven times more wall time. Whatever it is doing in there, it is not multiplying every token against every other token.

At the other end, GPT-4.1 nano random, restricted to its actual top-end range of ~78 k → ~611 k tokens, fits to α ≈ 1.16. That is superlinear — consistent with a regime where prefill cost has shifted from being dominated by some sub-quadratic prefix kernel into a regime where the per-token cost is itself growing with context. Whether that is dense attention belatedly showing up, or simply a routing change into a slower backend, is the question §4 is going to fail to answer.

The other thing the per-cell floors do, once you look closely, is move non-monotonically. Several models pop above their own trend at a specific context size and then settle back down at the next bucket. That pattern is the next section.

§3Humps, not staircases

TTFT does not rise as a clean staircase. It rises with humps — local elevations at characteristic context sizes that resolve at the next bucket up.

Stare at the per-cell averages long enough and the eye picks up something the power-law fits in §2 quietly average over: at certain context sizes, TTFT spikes above its own trend, then falls back to (or below) the trend at the next geometric bucket. The cleanest example is gemini-3.1-flash-lite on the song-toads prompt at 9 reps per cell: mean TTFT walks 1,675 → 2,266 → 1,201 ms across 62 k → 97 k → 144 k input tokens. The middle cell is 35 % above its right-hand neighbour, and the next cell up is 47 % below the peak. That is a hump.

Two patterns stand out across the table. First, each provider has its own characteristic hump zone: OpenAI models cluster their humps in the ~3–30 k input-token band, while Gemini models cluster theirs at ~30–100 k. The pattern shows up in different prompt shapes for the same model — same provider, same model, different prompt content, hump in the same place.

Second, the humps are highly reproducible at high n. The two cells in this table with 9 repeats each — Lite at 97 k and gpt-5.4-mini at 3 k — are not fluctuating around their reported peak. They are consistently elevated above the cells on either side. That is what makes the hump a feature of the system, not a sampling artifact.

§4What can produce a hump

Dense attention does not have favourite numbers. The hump is not architecture — it is operations.

A monotonically rising curve is what dense attention should produce: more context, more compute, more wall time. A hump — context grows, latency rises, then falls again — cannot come from the attention kernel itself. There is no operation in the model that becomes cheaper because the prompt got longer. So the hump has to come from somewhere outside the kernel: from routing, scheduling, caching, or some other piece of the serving stack that flips into a different mode at a particular size.

The mechanisms that fit the shape:

  • Routing-tier transition. The simplest story. Below the threshold, the request goes to the default pool, which was tuned for a different size class and handles it sub-optimally; above the threshold it goes to a long-context pool that is actually faster in absolute terms. The hump is the price you pay for being on the wrong side of the boundary. The drop at the next bucket is the new pool taking over. The fact that OpenAI models cluster their humps near ~30 k and Gemini models near ~100 k is consistent with each provider drawing the line in a different place.
  • Bimodal handling at a boundary. Variant of the above: at the boundary, individual requests get routed inconsistently — some hit the fast path, some the slow path. The peak cell shows elevated mean and a long tail; the cell on either side is tighter. This matches the Lite 97 k cell, where the per-call max is 6 × the min while neighbouring cells are within 2 ×.
  • Prefill-chunking sweet spot. Prefill is usually chunked into fixed-size pieces. At the size where the chunk count rises by one, you pay for pipeline-bubble or scheduling overhead that the next size up amortises away because its chunks are now the natural size for the new pipeline depth.
  • Cache-slab fragmentation. KV-cache allocators usually grab slabs in fixed sizes; allocating just past a slab boundary can force a copy or a fragmentation event that allocating well past it does not.

We do not have direct visibility into which of these is happening. We do have provider_request_id, system_fingerprint, and provider_model_version stamped on every row. The right follow-up is a dense repeat at the hump cell with sweep order randomised: if the fingerprint cleanly partitions the slow calls from the fast calls inside the same cell, the hump is bimodal routing. If the fingerprint is constant, the slowness is inside one pool — chunking or scheduling becomes the primary suspect.

§5A strange decode dip

For some models, decode-per-token falls as context grows. This is the wrong direction.

Once prefill finishes and the model starts emitting, every token it produces requires a fresh attention pass over the entire prompt-plus-output-so-far. A longer prompt means a larger KV cache to read on every step, which means — in the textbook — that decode_ms_per_token should be flat (cache size growing only on the output side, output is small) or gently increasing (if KV-cache reads scale visibly with prefix length). What it should not do is fall.

It falls.

The Lite case is the cleanest of these and the most provocative: a monotonic, three-step descent across a full 3× in context length. Per-token decode is nearly halving while the model has more KV cache to walk on every step than it did at the start. There is no honest reading of textbook attention that produces that shape. Something else is going on, and it is going on at a particular range of input sizes.

Candidates, in order of how badly we want them to be true

H1Speculative decoding kicks in past a routing threshold

Long-context routes are exactly the kind of expensive request that pays for speculative decoding most: you have a fat KV cache and you want to amortise cache-fetch cost across as many emitted tokens per scheduler step as possible. If the long-context tier runs a draft model that proposes 4–8 tokens per step and the target accepts most of them, observed decode_ms_per_token falls dramatically — not because the model got faster, but because more tokens come out per "step" the wall clock sees.

Sweep max_tokens wide on a fixed prompt across the threshold and inspect chunk_timestamps_ms. Speculative acceptance produces distinctively bursty inter-event timings (clusters of zero-ms gaps separated by full-step intervals); textbook one-token-at-a-time decode is uniform. A change in burstiness across the boundary is the signature.

H2The long-context tier is a different machine entirely

Closely related but mechanistically distinct: the long-context pool may run on different hardware (more memory bandwidth, larger TPU/GPU pods, different interconnect topology) where per-token costs are simply lower in absolute terms. The TTFT step we see in §3 would in this story be the cost of the routing change — queuing for and binding to the new pool — and the decode dip would be the benefit.

Cross-reference system_fingerprint / provider_model_version across the boundary in dense repeats. If the fingerprint changes coincident with the decode dip, we have direct evidence. If it does not, we are inside one pool and need a different story.

H3Streaming chunk coalescing distorts our denominator

Most boring possibility: provider streaming can buffer multiple emitted tokens into a single SSE chunk under load. We compute decode time as (last content delta − first content delta) / (output_tokens − 1), so if at long context the provider starts emitting larger chunks, our numerator shrinks (last content event arrives sooner relative to the actual end-of-decode) without any real speedup. This is an observability artifact, not a model phenomenon.

Compare against a non-streaming run with the same prompt & size. If the dip survives in total_ms − first_response_byte_ms normalised by output_tokens with streaming off, it is real. If it disappears, we were measuring chunk policy.

H4KV-cache layout optimises across some boundary

Above some prefix size the runtime may switch from a per-request KV layout optimised for short prompts (e.g., dense in HBM, addressed per-head) to a paged/blocked layout designed for long contexts. The latter has worse TTFT (the step we see) and better per-decode cache-read amortisation (the dip we see). This is internally consistent with both phenomena coming from a single switch.

Hold the prompt fixed, vary max_tokens from 16 up to 512, and check whether decode_ms_per_token drops at the same prefix-size boundary regardless of output length. If the boundary is purely about the prefix, this is the right story. If it depends on output length too, the mechanism is downstream of the cache layout.

None of these is testable from the dataset we already have. Each becomes testable with a small, directed follow-up sweep — which is roughly the work plan that falls out of this post.

§6What this is not

A short list of things this page does not claim, despite the temptation:

  • It does not claim that any specific model uses sparse attention. Sub-linear TTFT scaling is consistent with sparse attention and with prefill chunking, KV-cache reuse across requests, MoE routing, hardware tier changes, and several other things we cannot see from outside.
  • It does not claim the humps in §3 are stable across time. They are stable across the prompt shapes in this sweep, taken in randomised order over a few hours. A hump that disappears next week was probably a deploy.
  • It does not claim the decode dip in §5 is a property of the model itself, rather than a property of the path the request takes through the serving stack. H3 in particular is the "we measured a streaming policy" null hypothesis and has not been ruled out.

What this page does claim is that all seven of these models, on the floors where compute should dominate, scale much better than dense attention predicts; that several carry reproducible TTFT humps at provider-characteristic context sizes (OpenAI clusters near ~30 k, Gemini near ~100 k); and that decode time for several of them moves in the direction that should be impossible. The interesting work from here is small, sharp, and falsifiable. The interactive viewer is the place to slice the data however you like and check the claims yourself.
